Chapter 1

The Use of Have, Has, and Had

Learning Objectives
  • Understand what auxiliary (helping) verbs are
  • Learn the difference between main verbs and helping verbs
  • Master when to use have, has, and had
  • Apply correct subject-verb agreement
  • Form questions and negatives correctly
  • Recognise and fix common mistakes

What Are Helping Verbs?

In English, we have two types of verbs:

Two Types of Verbs

Main Verbs — action words that tell us what someone does: run, eat, sleep, walk, play

Helping Verbs (Auxiliary Verbs) — verbs that help the main verb and come before it: is running, has eaten, will play

Understanding Helping Verbs

A helping verb works together with a main verb to create meaning. Think of it as the main verb's assistant.

Full SentenceHelping VerbMain Verb
She is runningisrunning
They have finishedhavefinished
We will gowillgo
He had lefthadleft

The Three Forms

Think of have, has, and had as the same verb in different forms:

  • Have — present tense for I, you, we, they
  • Has — present tense for he, she, it
  • Had — past tense for all subjects

This is called subject-verb agreement — the verb must match the subject.

Memory Trick

HAVE = for I, you, we, they (more than one or first/second person)
HAS = only for he, she, it (one person or thing)

When to Use HAVE

Use have with: I, you, we, they, and plural nouns.

Examples with HAVE
I have a pen.
You have done excellent work.
We have completed the project.
They have arrived at the station.
The students have finished their homework.

When to Use HAS

Use has only with: he, she, it, and singular nouns.

Examples with HAS
He has a new car.
She has completed the race.
It has been raining all day.
Aisha has finished her project.
The dog has eaten its food.
Key Rule

If you can replace the subject with he, she, or it, use HAS. Otherwise, use HAVE.

When to Use HAD

Had is the past tense form. It works with all subjects — no exceptions.

Examples with HAD
I had finished before they arrived.
She had completed the work.
They had arrived early.
The team had won three times before.

Because had stays the same for all subjects, it is the easiest of the three!

Perfect Tenses

Have, has, and had are used to create perfect tenses — tenses that connect one event to another in time.

TenseHelping VerbExampleMeaning
Present Perfecthave / hasI have eatenStarted in past, connected to now
Past PerfecthadI had eatenHappened before another past event

Forming Negatives

Add not after the helping verb to make a sentence negative:

Negative Forms
I have not finished → I haven't finished
She has not arrived → She hasn't arrived
They had not called → They hadn't called

Forming Questions

Move the helping verb to the beginning of the sentence:

Questions

Statement: You have finished your work.

Question: Have you finished your work?

Statement: She has arrived.

Question: Has she arrived?

Short Answers

Short Answer Examples

Q: Have you eaten?
A: Yes, I have. / No, I haven't.

Q: Has he finished?
A: Yes, he has. / No, he hasn't.

Q: Had they arrived?
A: Yes, they had. / No, they hadn't.

Common Mistake

Wrong: "He have finished." / "She have arrived."

Correct: "He has finished." / "She has arrived."

He, she, and it always need HAS, never HAVE.

Common Mistakes

Mistake 1 — Wrong form with singular noun

Wrong: "The student have finished."

Correct: "The student has finished." — singular noun needs HAS.

Mistake 2 — Wrong form with plural noun

Wrong: "The students has finished."

Correct: "The students have finished." — plural noun needs HAVE.

Mistake 3 — Wrong tense with time word

Wrong: "I have finished yesterday."

Correct: "I finished yesterday." — use simple past with a specific past time.

Quick Reference Chart

SubjectPresentPastExample
IhavehadI have gone
YouhavehadYou have gone
He / She / IthashadShe has gone
WehavehadWe have gone
TheyhavehadThey have gone
Practice — Fill in the Correct Form
Question 1

She _____ (have / has) a beautiful garden.

has — She is singular, so we use has.
Question 2

We _____ (have / has) been friends for five years.

have — We is plural, so we use have.
Question 3

The children _____ (have / has) finished their dinner.

have — Children is plural, so we use have.
Question 4

It _____ (have / has) been raining since morning.

has — It is singular, so we use has.
Question 5

had — The eating happened before another past event (the phone call), so we use had.
Chapter 1 Summary
Have, has, and had are auxiliary (helping) verbs.
Use have with I, you, we, they and plural nouns.
Use has with he, she, it and singular nouns.
Use had for all subjects in the past.
To make negatives, add "not" after the helping verb.
To make questions, move the helping verb to the beginning.
Chapter 2

Antonyms and Synonyms

Learning Objectives
  • Understand what synonyms are
  • Understand what antonyms are
  • Identify both in context
  • Use them to improve your writing
  • Expand your vocabulary naturally

What Are Synonyms?

Synonyms are words that have very similar meanings. They look and sound different but mean roughly the same thing.

Synonym Examples
Happy = Joyful, Cheerful, Glad
Big = Large, Huge, Enormous
Fast = Quick, Rapid, Swift
Sad = Unhappy, Sorrowful, Depressed

What Are Antonyms?

Antonyms are words that have opposite meanings.

Antonym Examples
Hot ≠ Cold
Big ≠ Small
Fast ≠ Slow
Happy ≠ Sad
Easy Way to Remember

Synonym — S for Same. Synonyms have similar meanings.

Antonym — A for Against. Antonyms are against each other (opposites).

Why They Both Matter

TypeMeaningExample
SynonymSame or similarHappy, Joyful, Glad
AntonymOppositeHappy ≠ Sad

Common Synonyms

WordSynonyms (weakest to strongest)
GoodNice, Fine, Excellent, Outstanding, Superb
BadPoor, Terrible, Awful, Horrible, Abysmal
HappyPleased, Joyful, Delighted, Thrilled, Ecstatic
SadUnhappy, Gloomy, Sorrowful, Miserable, Devastated
SmartClever, Intelligent, Brilliant, Genius
TiredWeary, Exhausted, Drained, Fatigued
BeautifulPretty, Lovely, Gorgeous, Stunning, Breathtaking

Common Antonyms

WordAntonym (opposite)
HotCold
Big / LargeSmall / Tiny
Fast / QuickSlow
HappySad
BeautifulUgly
DayNight
YoungOld
BeginningEnd

Synonyms Have Different Strengths

Synonyms can express different intensities of the same feeling:

Strength Scale
Weak: "I like chocolate."
Medium: "I enjoy chocolate."
Strong: "I love chocolate."
Choosing the Right Synonym
  • Is this formal or casual writing?
  • Do I want a strong or mild meaning?
  • Does the exact meaning fit this sentence?
  • Only use words you fully understand.

Using Synonyms to Improve Writing

Before (Repetitive)
"The story was good. The characters were good. The ending was good."
After (Using Synonyms)
"The story was excellent. The characters were wonderful. The ending was satisfying."
Be Careful

Wrong: "The explanation was pellucid." (if you don't know what pellucid means)

Better: "The explanation was clear." — a simple word used correctly beats a fancy word used wrongly.

Rewrite with Synonyms

"The children were happy. They ran fast. They were happy to play in the big park."

"The children were delighted. They sprinted quickly. They were thrilled to play in the spacious park." (Your answer may vary, but avoid repeating "happy" and "big")
Chapter 2 Summary
Synonyms = words with the same or similar meanings.
Antonyms = words with opposite meanings.
Synonyms help you avoid repetition in writing.
Synonyms can have different strengths or intensities.
Always use words you fully understand.
Antonyms help show contrast and comparison.
Chapter 3

Causative Verbs

Learning Objectives
  • Understand what causation means
  • Learn the difference between doing something yourself and arranging for it
  • Master have, get, make, let, and help
  • Form causative sentences correctly

What Is Causation?

Causation means to cause or arrange for something to happen. A causative verb shows that the subject makes another person do something — or arranges for it to be done.

The Key Difference

YOU do it: "I washed the car." (you washed it yourself)

You HAVE someone else do it: "I had the car washed." (someone else washed it for you)

Regular ActionCausative Action
I cut my hair.I had my hair cut. (by a barber)
She baked the cake.She had the cake baked. (by someone else)
He fixed the car.He had the car fixed. (by a mechanic)
Key Concept

Causative verbs show that you made something happen — but you did not do the action yourself.

The Five Causative Verbs

  1. HAVE
  2. GET
  3. MAKE
  4. LET
  5. HELP

HAVE and GET

Structure: have / get + object + past participle

Both mean you arranged for someone else to do something. GET is more informal.

HAVE Examples
I have my shoes cleaned. (someone cleans them)
She has her car repaired. (a mechanic repairs it)
They had their house painted. (a painter did it)
GET Examples
I got my teeth cleaned. (at the dentist)
She got her hair dyed. (at the salon)

MAKE

Structure: make + object + base verb (no "to")

You force or require someone to do something.

MAKE Examples
The teacher made the students write an essay.
My mother made me clean my room.

LET

Structure: let + object + base verb (no "to")

You permit or allow someone to do something.

LET Examples
The parents let their children play outside.
The manager let me leave early.

HELP

Structure: help + object + (to) base verb

You assist someone. The "to" is optional with HELP.

HELP Examples
I helped him fix the car.
I helped him to fix the car. (both are correct)
Common Mistake

Wrong: "I made him to go." / "She let me to leave."

Correct: "I made him go." / "She let me leave." — never use "to" with MAKE or LET.

Comparison Chart

VerbStructureMeaning
HAVE+ past participleArrange / pay someone
GET+ past participleArrange (informal)
MAKE+ base verbForce someone
LET+ base verbAllow someone
HELP+ (to) base verbAssist someone
Practice — Choose the Correct Verb
Question 1

The teacher _____ the students complete their assignments. (made / let / had)

made — The teacher forced the students to complete them.
Question 2

I _____ my suit cleaned at the dry cleaners. (made / let / had)

had — I arranged for someone to clean the suit.
Question 3

The coach _____ the players take a break after practice. (made / let / helped)

let — The coach allowed the players to rest.
Question 4 — Correct the Sentence

Wrong: "I made him to finish his homework."

Correct: "I made him finish his homework." — remove "to" with MAKE.
Chapter 3 Summary
Causative verbs show you made something happen.
HAVE / GET — arrange for someone else to do it.
MAKE — force someone to do it.
LET — allow someone to do it.
HELP — assist someone in doing it.
MAKE and LET never use "to" before the verb.
HAVE and GET use the past participle form.
Chapter 4

Adverbs of Place and Composition

Learning Objectives
  • Understand what adverbs of place are
  • Learn where they appear in sentences
  • Master movement and location vocabulary
  • Understand the structure of a composition
  • Learn to organise ideas clearly

What Are Adverbs of Place?

An adverb of place tells you WHERE something happens. It describes the location, direction, or position of an action.

LocationDirectionPosition
Here, There, WhereUp, Down, Forward, BackwardInside, Outside, Above, Below
Home, AbroadLeft, Right, Towards, AwayIn, Out, Near, Far
Adverbs of Place in Sentences
She sat here waiting for the bus. (location)
The ball rolled down the hill. (direction)
The bird flew above the clouds. (position)
They drove towards the mountains. (movement)

Position in a Sentence

Adverbs of place usually go at the end of a sentence, or at the beginning for emphasis:

Placement
End (most common): "The children were playing outside."
Beginning (emphasis): "Outside, the children were playing."
Remember

Adverbs of place answer the question: WHERE?

What Is a Composition?

A composition is a written piece that expresses ideas, tells a story, describes something, or argues a point — in an organised way.

Basic Structure

All good compositions have three parts:

The Three-Part Structure

1. Introduction — introduces the topic, grabs attention (2–3 sentences)

2. Body — develops the main ideas (2–4 paragraphs, one idea each)

3. Conclusion — summarises and leaves a final impression (2–3 sentences)

Types of Compositions

1. Narrative

Tells a story with characters and events in time order.

Narrative Example
"Last summer, I had the most exciting adventure at the beach..."
"First, we built a sandcastle. Then, we saw dolphins..."
"It was the best day, and I'll never forget those memories."

2. Descriptive

Paints a picture with words — describing how something looks, sounds, or feels.

Descriptive Example
"My grandmother's kitchen is the heart of her home."
"The walls are a warm cream. Fresh flowers sit on the windowsill..."
"Every time I enter, I feel welcomed and loved."

3. Expository

Explains or informs using facts and details about a topic.

4. Argumentative

Persuades the reader to agree with your opinion.

The Writing Process
  • Plan — brainstorm and organise your ideas
  • Draft — write without stopping to fix mistakes
  • Edit — check structure, flow, and content
  • Proofread — fix grammar, spelling, and punctuation

Using Transitions

Transitions connect your ideas and make your composition flow smoothly:

PurposeWords to Use
Add ideasAlso, Furthermore, In addition, Moreover
Show timeFirst, Next, Then, Finally, After, Later
Show contrastHowever, But, Although, On the other hand
Cause / EffectBecause, Therefore, As a result, Since

Common Mistakes in Composition

No Introduction

Never jump straight into events. Start with 2–3 sentences introducing your topic.

Everything in One Paragraph

Create a new paragraph for each new main idea or event.

No Conclusion

Always wrap up with 2–3 sentences that close your composition.

Practice — Plan a Narrative
Topic: A Day I Will Never Forget

Answer these planning questions:
1. Who are the main characters?
2. Where does the story take place?
3. What are the main events (in order)?
4. What is the ending?

1. Me and my best friend
2. The amusement park
3. We arrived early → rode the roller coaster → got lost → found the exit
4. We had an amazing adventure and laughed about getting lost
Chapter 4 Summary
Adverbs of place answer WHERE.
They show location, direction, or position.
They usually go at the end of a sentence.
A composition has Introduction, Body, and Conclusion.
Four types: Narrative, Descriptive, Expository, Argumentative.
Use the Writing Process: Plan, Draft, Edit, Proofread.
Chapter 5

Prefixes and Suffixes

Learning Objectives
  • Understand what prefixes and suffixes are
  • Learn common prefixes and their meanings
  • Learn common suffixes and their meanings
  • Recognise spelling changes when adding affixes
  • Build vocabulary by understanding word parts

What Are Affixes?

An affix is a word part added to a root word to create a new word with a different meaning.

Prefix vs Suffix

Prefix — added at the BEGINNING: un + happy = unhappy

Suffix — added at the END: happy + ly = happily

Common Prefixes

PrefixMeaningExamples
UN-Notunhappy, unkind, unclear
RE-Againrebuild, rewrite, restart
PRE-Beforepreview, precook, preschool
DIS-Not / oppositedisagree, dishonest, dislike
MIS-Wrongmistake, misunderstand, misplace
IN- / IM-Notincomplete, impossible, incorrect
NON-Notnonfiction, nonstop, nonprofit
SUB-Under / belowsubway, submarine, subtitle
Remember

Prefixes come at the BEGINNING and usually make the word negative or give it an opposite meaning.

Common Suffixes

A suffix changes the meaning and often the part of speech of a word:

SuffixPart of SpeechMeaningExamples
-TION / -SIONNounAction / Statecelebration, decision
-MENTNounState / Qualityenjoyment, movement
-NESSNounState / Qualityhappiness, kindness
-FULAdjectiveFull ofbeautiful, helpful
-LESSAdjectiveWithouthopeless, careless
-LYAdverbIn a wayquickly, happily
-ABLE / -IBLEAdjectiveCan be donereadable, possible
-ER / -ORNounPerson who doesteacher, actor
-ISHAdjectiveSomewhat likechildish, foolish
-FUL vs -LESS
Hope + ful = Hopeful (full of hope)
Hope + less = Hopeless (without hope)
Care + ful = Careful (full of care)
Care + less = Careless (without care)
-ER Suffix
Teach + er = Teacher (one who teaches)
Play + er = Player (one who plays)
Write + r = Writer (one who writes)
Remember

Suffixes come at the END and usually change the part of speech or add information about meaning.

Spelling Rules When Adding Suffixes

Rule 1 — Silent E

When a suffix starts with a vowel, drop the final "e" from the root word:

Silent E Rule
Hope + ing = Hoping (drop the e)
Smile + ing = Smiling (drop the e)
Fine + ly = Finely (keep the e — suffix starts with consonant)

Rule 2 — Double the Consonant

When a short vowel is followed by one consonant, double the consonant before adding a vowel suffix:

Doubling Rule
Run + ing = Running (double the n)
Stop + ing = Stopping (double the p)
Hot + er = Hotter (double the t)

Rule 3 — Y to I

When a word ends in "y", change it to "i" before most suffixes:

Y to I Rule
Happy + ness = Happiness (y → i)
Heavy + ly = Heavily (y → i)
Study + ing = Studying (y stays before -ing)

Guessing Unknown Words

Break any unknown word into parts to guess its meaning:

Breaking Down Words

UNCOMFORTABLE
UN (not) + COMFORT + ABLE (can be) = Not able to be comfortable

DISAGREEMENT
DIS (opposite) + AGREE + MENT (state) = The state of not agreeing

UNHELPFULLY
UN + HELP + FUL + LY = In an unhelpful way

Strategy for Unknown Words
  1. Look for a prefix at the beginning
  2. Identify the root word
  3. Look for a suffix at the end
  4. Combine the meanings
  5. Check if your guess makes sense in context

Word Families

RootWith PrefixWith SuffixWith Both
HappyUnhappyHappinessUnhappily
ClearUnclearClearlyUnclearly
LikeDislikeLikeableDislikeable
BuildRebuildBuildingRebuilding
AgreeDisagreeAgreementDisagreement

Chapter 5 Summary
Prefixes are added at the BEGINNING of words.
Suffixes are added at the END of words.
Common prefixes: UN-, RE-, PRE-, DIS-, MIS-, IN-, NON-, SUB-.
Common suffixes: -TION, -NESS, -FUL, -LESS, -LY, -ER, -ABLE, -MENT.
Follow the three spelling rules when adding suffixes.
Break unknown words into parts to guess their meaning.

Question 1

(a) Solve for x: 3(2x − 5) − 2(x − 4) = 15

(b) The sum of three consecutive whole numbers is 84. Find the three numbers.

Incorrect code. Please try again.
Step-by-Step Solution
Part (a): Expand both brackets first.
3(2x − 5) = 6x − 15, and 2(x − 4) = 2x − 8
So the equation becomes: 6x − 15 − (2x − 8) = 15
Subtracting a bracket flips every sign inside it: 6x − 15 − 2x + 8 = 15
Collect like terms: (6x − 2x) + (−15 + 8) = 15 → 4x − 7 = 15
Add 7 to both sides: 4x = 22
Divide both sides by 4: x = 5.5
Part (b): Let the smallest number be n. Since the numbers are consecutive, they are n, n+1, and n+2.
Their sum is: n + (n+1) + (n+2) = 84
Simplify: 3n + 3 = 84
Subtract 3: 3n = 81
Divide by 3: n = 27
The three numbers are 27, 28, and 29.
Question 2

In the diagram below, lines PQ and RS are parallel straight lines cut by a transversal line. The co-interior (allied) angles formed are (2x + 10)° and (3x − 30)°.

(a) State the theorem that connects these two angles.

(b) Use the theorem to find the value of x.

(c) Find the actual size of each angle.

Incorrect code. Please try again.
Step-by-Step Solution
Part (a): When a transversal cuts two parallel lines, co-interior angles lie between the parallel lines, on the same side of the transversal. The theorem states that co-interior angles are supplementary — they add up to 180°.
Part (b): (2x + 10) + (3x − 30) = 180
Collect like terms: 5x − 20 = 180
Add 20 to both sides: 5x = 200
Divide by 5: x = 40
Part (c): Substitute x = 40 into each expression.
First angle = 2(40) + 10 = 90°
Second angle = 3(40) − 30 = 90°
Check: 90° + 90° = 180° ✓ — this confirms the answer is correct.
Question 3

A rectangular field measures 45m long and 30m wide. A path of uniform width 2.5m is constructed all around the outside of the field.

(a) Find the area of the field.

(b) Find the area of the path.

(c) If tarring the path costs ₦2,500 per square metre, find the total cost of tarring it.

Incorrect code. Please try again.
Step-by-Step Solution
Part (a): Area of a rectangle = length × width = 45 × 30 = 1,350 m²
Part (b): Because the path surrounds the field, it adds 2.5m to every side. Outer length = 45 + 2.5 + 2.5 = 50m. Outer width = 30 + 2.5 + 2.5 = 35m.
Outer area = 50 × 35 = 1,750 m²
Area of path = Outer area − Field area = 1,750 − 1,350 = 400 m²
Part (c): Cost = 400 × ₦2,500 = ₦1,000,000
Question 4

A cylindrical tank has a radius of 7cm and a height of 20cm. (Take π = 22/7)

(a) Calculate the volume of the cylinder.

(b) A cone with the same radius and height is carved from an identical cylindrical block. Calculate the volume of the cone.

(c) Find the difference between the two volumes.

Incorrect code. Please try again.
Step-by-Step Solution
Part (a): Volume of a cylinder = πr²h = 22/7 × 7 × 7 × 20
The 7 in the denominator cancels with one of the r's: 22 × 7 × 20 = 3,080 cm³
Part (b): Volume of a cone = ⅓ × πr²h = ⅓ × 3,080
= 3,080 ÷ 3 = 1,026.67 cm³ (2 d.p.)
Part (c): Difference = 3,080 − 1,026.67 = 2,053.33 cm³
This makes sense — a cone always has exactly ⅓ the volume of a cylinder sharing the same base and height.
Question 5

The scores of 10 students in a Mathematics test (out of 20) are:
12, 15, 18, 10, 14, 16, 12, 19, 13, 11

(a) Find the mean score.

(b) Find the median score.

(c) Find the mode.

Incorrect code. Please try again.
Step-by-Step Solution
Part (a) — Mean: Add up all 10 scores: 12+15+18+10+14+16+12+19+13+11 = 140
Mean = 140 ÷ 10 = 14
Part (b) — Median: Arrange in order: 10, 11, 12, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 18, 19
With 10 values (even), the median is the average of the 5th and 6th values: (13 + 14) ÷ 2 = 13.5
Part (c) — Mode: 12 appears twice; every other value appears once. Mode = 12

JSS 2 — Third Term Theory Questions

Question 1

The interior angles of a pentagon are (x + 10)°, (x + 20)°, (x + 30)°, (x + 50)°, and (x + 70)°.

(a) Find the value of x.

(b) Find the size of each angle.

Incorrect code. Please try again.
Step-by-Step Solution
Part (a): The sum of interior angles of any polygon = (n − 2) × 180°, where n = number of sides. For a pentagon, n = 5.
Sum = (5 − 2) × 180 = 3 × 180 = 540°
Add all five angle expressions and set equal to 540: (x+10)+(x+20)+(x+30)+(x+50)+(x+70) = 540
Collect like terms: 5x + 180 = 540
Subtract 180: 5x = 360
Divide by 5: x = 72
Part (b): Substitute x = 72 into each expression:
72+10 = 82°, 72+20 = 92°, 72+30 = 102°, 72+50 = 122°, 72+70 = 142°
Check: 82+92+102+122+142 = 540° ✓
Question 2

From the top of a vertical tower 45m high, the angle of depression of a car on the ground is 32°.

Calculate the distance of the car from the foot of the tower, correct to 1 decimal place.

Incorrect code. Please try again.
Step-by-Step Solution
The angle of depression (measured from the horizontal line of sight down to the car) is always equal to the angle of elevation (measured from the car up to the top of the tower) — this is because the horizontal line and the ground are parallel, making these alternate angles equal. So the angle inside our triangle, at the car, is also 32°.
We now have a right-angled triangle: the tower (45m) is the side "opposite" the 32° angle, and the distance from the car to the tower's foot is the side "adjacent" to it.
tan(angle) = opposite ÷ adjacent, so: tan(32°) = 45 ÷ distance
Rearranging: distance = 45 ÷ tan(32°)
tan(32°) ≈ 0.6249
distance = 45 ÷ 0.6249 ≈ 72.0m (1 d.p.)
Question 3

A cyclist rides from point X, 15km due north, to point Y. He then rides 20km due east to point Z.

(a) Find the distance XZ, correct to 1 decimal place.

(b) Find the bearing of Z from X, correct to the nearest degree.

Incorrect code. Please try again.
Step-by-Step Solution
Part (a): Since X to Y is due north and Y to Z is due east, angle XYZ is a right angle (90°). This means triangle XYZ is right-angled, so we can use Pythagoras' Theorem.
XZ² = XY² + YZ² = 15² + 20² = 225 + 400 = 625
XZ = √625 = 25.0km
Part (b): Bearing is always measured clockwise from north. At point X, north points straight up towards Y. We need the angle between north (XY) and the line XZ.
In the right-angled triangle, tan(angle YXZ) = opposite ÷ adjacent = YZ ÷ XY = 20 ÷ 15 = 1.3333
angle YXZ = tan⁻¹(1.3333) ≈ 53.13°
Since this angle is measured from north (the XY direction) turning clockwise towards Z (which is to the east), the bearing of Z from X = 053° (to the nearest degree).

The table shows the number of goals scored by a football team in 20 matches during a season:

Goals scored (x)012345

(a) Find the mean number of goals scored per match.

(b) Find the median number of goals.

Incorrect code. Please try again.
Step-by-Step Solution
Part (a) — Mean: First check the total number of matches: 2+5+6+4+2+1 = 20 ✓ (matches the question)

Question 5

A ladder 13m long leans against a vertical wall such that the foot of the ladder is 5m away from the base of the wall.

(a) Find how high up the wall the ladder reaches.

(b) If the foot of the ladder is now pulled 2m further away from the wall, find the new height reached, correct to 2 decimal places.

Incorrect code. Please try again.
Step-by-Step Solution
Part (a): The ladder, wall, and ground form a right-angled triangle — the ladder is the hypotenuse (13m), the distance from the wall (5m) is one leg, and the height on the wall is the other leg.
By Pythagoras' Theorem: (hypotenuse)² = (leg 1)² + (leg 2)²
13² = 5² + height² → 169 = 25 + height²
height² = 169 − 25 = 144
height = √144 = 12m
Part (b): New distance from the wall = 5 + 2 = 7m. The ladder length (13m) does not change.
13² = 7² + new height² → 169 = 49 + new height²
new height² = 169 − 49 = 120
new height = √120 ≈ 10.95m (2 d.p.)
Notice the ladder reaches lower up the wall (12m → 10.95m) once its foot is pulled further out — this always happens with a fixed-length ladder.

Question 1

The sum of two numbers is 24. Three times the first number minus the second number is 28.

(a) Write two equations representing this information.

(b) Solve the equations simultaneously to find the two numbers.

Incorrect code. Please try again.
Step-by-Step Solution
Part (a): Let the first number be x and the second number be y.
"The sum of two numbers is 24" → Equation (i): x + y = 24
"Three times the first minus the second is 28" → Equation (ii): 3x − y = 28
Part (b): Notice that equation (i) has "+y" and equation (ii) has "−y" — if we add the two equations together, the y-terms will cancel out.
(i) + (ii): (x + y) + (3x − y) = 24 + 28
x + 3x + y − y = 52 → 4x = 52
Divide by 4: x = 13
Substitute x = 13 into equation (i): 13 + y = 24 → y = 11
Check in equation (ii): 3(13) − 11 = 39 − 11 = 28 ✓
The two numbers are 13 and 11.

(a) The ages (in years) of 9 members of a club are: 22, 25, 19, 30, 25, 28, 22, 25, 35. Find the mean, median, and mode of their ages.

Mean = 231 ÷ 9 = 25.67 years (2 d.p.)

Mode = the age that appears most often = 25 years (it appears 3 times).
Part (b) — Grouped Data: For grouped data, we use the midpoint of each class (called the "class mark") to estimate the mean, and we work out a running total (cumulative frequency).

Question 3

y varies directly as x and inversely as the square of z. When x = 4 and z = 2, y = 6.

(a) Find the equation connecting x, y, and z.

(b) Find y when x = 9 and z = 3.

Incorrect code. Please try again.
Step-by-Step Solution
Part (a): "Varies directly as x" means y gets bigger when x gets bigger (y ∝ x). "Varies inversely as the square of z" means y gets smaller when z² gets bigger (y ∝ 1/z²). Combining both: y ∝ x/z², which we write as an equation using a constant k: y = kx/z²
Substitute the known values (x=4, z=2, y=6) to find k: 6 = k(4)/(2²) = 4k/4 = k
So k = 6, and the equation is: y = 6x/z²
Part (b): Substitute x = 9 and z = 3 into the equation: y = 6(9)/(3²) = 54/9 = 6
Question 4

A ship sails from a port A on a bearing of N50°E for 70km to a point B. From B, it changes course and sails on a bearing of S60°E for 90km to a point C.

(a) Find the distance AC, correct to 1 decimal place.

(b) Find the bearing of C from A, correct to the nearest degree.

Incorrect code. Please try again.
Step-by-Step Solution
Step 1 — Find angle ABC: The bearing from A to B is N50°E, so the reverse bearing (from B back to A) is S50°W. The bearing from B to C is S60°E.
Both reverse-bearings are measured from south at B, one swinging 50° to the west, the other 60° to the east — so the angle between them (angle ABC) = 50° + 60° = 110°.
Part (a): Since we now know two sides (AB=70km, BC=90km) and the angle between them (110°), we use the Cosine Rule to find AC.
AC² = AB² + BC² − 2(AB)(BC)cos(B)
AC² = 70² + 90² − 2(70)(90)cos(110°)
AC² = 4900 + 8100 − 12600 × (−0.3420)
AC² = 13000 + 4309.2 = 17309.2
AC = √17309.2 ≈ 131.6km (1 d.p.)
Part (b): To find the bearing of C from A, first find angle BAC using the Sine Rule.
sin(BAC) ÷ BC = sin(B) ÷ AC → sin(BAC) = [90 × sin(110°)] ÷ 131.6 = [90 × 0.9397] ÷ 131.6 ≈ 0.6428
angle BAC = sin⁻¹(0.6428) ≈ 40°
The bearing of B from A is N50°E (i.e. 050°). Since C lies further round (to the east side) as seen from A, we add angle BAC to this bearing: 050° + 40° = 090°
Question 5

P varies jointly as Q and the square root of R. When Q = 4 and R = 9, P = 48.

(a) Find the equation connecting P, Q, and R.

(b) Find P when Q = 6 and R = 25.

Incorrect code. Please try again.
Step-by-Step Solution
Part (a): "Varies jointly as Q and the square root of R" means P depends on both Q and √R multiplied together: P ∝ Q√R, so P = kQ√R for some constant k.
Substitute the known values (Q=4, R=9, P=48): 48 = k(4)(√9) = k(4)(3) = 12k
Divide both sides by 12: k = 4
The equation is: P = 4Q√R
Part (b): Substitute Q = 6 and R = 25: P = 4(6)(√25) = 4(6)(5) = 120
