Chapter 1

Prisms — Triangular and Rectangular

Learning Objectives
  • Understand what a prism is and its parts
  • Trace a ray of light through a triangular prism
  • Understand refraction at each surface
  • Know the conditions for minimum deviation
  • Understand total internal reflection in a rectangular prism
  • Know the practical uses of each prism type

What Is a Prism?

A prism is a transparent solid object with flat, polished surfaces through which light can pass and be refracted (bent). The surfaces are called faces. Prisms are used in many optical instruments to bend, reflect, or disperse light.

The Triangular Prism

A triangular prism has a cross-section that is a triangle. It has three rectangular faces and two triangular ends. The important parts to know are:

  • Refracting faces — the two faces through which light enters and exits
  • Refracting edge — the edge where the two refracting faces meet
  • Apex angle (A) — the angle at the top between the two refracting faces. Also called the angle of the prism
  • Base — the bottom face, opposite the apex

How Light Travels Through a Triangular Prism

When a ray of light enters a triangular prism, it undergoes refraction twice — once when entering and once when leaving. At each surface, the ray bends because it moves from one medium (air) into another (glass) and back again.

Step-by-Step Ray Tracing

Step 1: The ray hits the first face and bends towards the normal (air to glass — it slows down).

Step 2: The ray travels through the glass.

Step 3: The ray hits the second face and bends away from the normal (glass to air — it speeds up).

Result: The emergent ray is NOT parallel to the incident ray. It has been deviated (turned) towards the base of the prism.

Key Rule

Light passing through a triangular prism always bends towards the base of the prism, regardless of which face it enters.

Important Angles in a Triangular Prism

r₂Angle of incidence (second face)Angle inside the prism at second face

Important Relationship

Inside the prism: r₁ + r₂ = A

Total deviation: D = (i + e) - A

Minimum Deviation

As you rotate the prism (change the angle of incidence), the angle of deviation first decreases, reaches a minimum value (Dₐ), then increases again. The minimum deviation is a very important condition.

At minimum deviation:

  • The ray travels symmetrically through the prism
  • The angle of incidence equals the angle of emergence: i = e
  • The refracted ray inside is parallel to the base: r₁ = r₂ = r
  • The refractive index can be calculated from the formula below
Refractive Index Formula (Minimum Deviation)

n = sin[(A + Dₐ) / 2] / sin[A / 2]

The Rectangular Glass Prism

A rectangular glass prism (also called a right-angled prism) is a prism with angles of 45°, 45°, and 90°. It is one of the most useful optical components because it uses total internal reflection to reflect light perfectly — better than any mirror.

What Is Total Internal Reflection (TIR)?

Total internal reflection occurs when light tries to pass from a denser medium (glass) to a less dense medium (air), but the angle of incidence is greater than or equal to the critical angle. Instead of passing through, all the light is reflected back into the glass — with no loss of intensity.

Two Conditions for TIR
  • Light must travel from denser to less dense medium (glass to air)
  • The angle of incidence must be greater than or equal to the critical angle (C)

The Critical Angle

The critical angle C is the angle of incidence in the denser medium that gives an angle of refraction of exactly 90 degrees in the less dense medium. The relationship with refractive index n is:

Critical Angle Formula

sin C = 1/n   or   n = 1/sin C

For glass with n = 1.5, sin C = 1/1.5 = 0.667, so C = 42°. Since the right-angled prism hits the hypotenuse face at 45°, which is greater than 42°, TIR always occurs.

How a Rectangular Prism Uses TIR

UseHow It WorksApplication
Turn a ray 90°Light enters one short face, TIR at hypotenuse, exits other short facePeriscopes, binoculars
Turn a ray 180°Two TIR reflections redirect ray back the way it cameRetroreflectors, cats-eye road studs
Invert an imagePorro prism arrangement flips image upside down and left-rightBinoculars, telescopes

Advantages of Prisms Over Mirrors

  • A mirror reflects only about 90–95% of light; a prism using TIR reflects 100%
  • Prisms do not tarnish, corrode, or lose their reflective quality over time
  • Prisms produce a sharper image
UTME / WAEC Practice — Prisms
Question 1 (UTME-Style)

The angle of a triangular prism is 60°. At minimum deviation, the angle of incidence is 45°. Calculate the refractive index of the prism material.

Given: A = 60°, at minimum deviation i = e, so Dₐ = 2i - A = 2(45) - 60 = 30°

Formula: n = sin[(A + Dₐ)/2] / sin[A/2]

n = sin[(60 + 30)/2] / sin[60/2]
n = sin 45° / sin 30°
n = 0.7071 / 0.5
n = 1.41
Question 2 (WAEC-Style)

The critical angle for a glass-air interface is 42°. Calculate the refractive index of the glass. If a ray strikes a surface of this glass at an angle of incidence of 50° from inside, will total internal reflection occur? Give a reason.

n = 1/sin C = 1/sin 42° = 1/0.6691 = 1.49

50° > 42° (critical angle), so yes, total internal reflection will occur because the angle of incidence exceeds the critical angle.
Question 3 (Hard — WAEC)

A triangular prism has an apex angle of 50°. A ray passes through at minimum deviation. The refractive index of the prism is 1.60. Calculate: (a) the angle of refraction inside the prism, (b) the angle of minimum deviation, and (c) the angle of incidence.

(a) At minimum deviation, r₁ = r₂ = A/2 = 50/2 = 25°

(b) n = sin[(A + Dₐ)/2] / sin[A/2]
1.60 = sin[(50 + Dₐ)/2] / sin 25°
sin[(50 + Dₐ)/2] = 1.60 × sin 25° = 1.60 × 0.4226 = 0.6762
(50 + Dₐ)/2 = sin⁻¹(0.6762) = 42.5°
50 + Dₐ = 85°
Dₐ = 35°

(c) Dₐ = 2i - A → 35 = 2i - 50 → 2i = 85 → i = 42.5°
Question 4 (Hard — UTME)

Light passes through a glass prism (n = 1.52) with apex angle 60°. Calculate the angle of minimum deviation and the speed of light in the glass. (Speed of light in air = 3.0 × 10⁸ m/s)

Minimum deviation:
n = sin[(A + Dₐ)/2] / sin[A/2]
1.52 = sin[(60 + Dₐ)/2] / sin 30°
sin[(60 + Dₐ)/2] = 1.52 × 0.5 = 0.76
(60 + Dₐ)/2 = 49.46°
60 + Dₐ = 98.92°
Dₐ = 38.9° ≈ 39°

Speed in glass:
n = c/v → v = c/n = (3.0 × 10⁸)/1.52
v = 1.97 × 10⁸ m/s
Chapter 1 Summary
A triangular prism bends light towards its base through double refraction.
Apex angle A, deviation D, and incidence i are related: D = (i + e) - A.
At minimum deviation: i = e, r₁ = r₂, and n = sin[(A+Dₐ)/2] / sin(A/2).
A rectangular prism uses total internal reflection at the hypotenuse.
TIR requires: denser to less dense medium AND angle ≥ critical angle.
Critical angle: sin C = 1/n. For glass (n=1.5), C = 42°.
Chapter 2

Angle of Deviation, Real Depth and Apparent Depth

Learning Objectives
  • Define and calculate the angle of deviation in a prism
  • Understand how deviation changes with angle of incidence
  • Understand what apparent depth means and why it occurs
  • Solve problems involving real depth and apparent depth
  • Calculate the refractive index from depth measurements

Angle of Deviation — A Full Treatment

The angle of deviation (D) is the angle between the original direction of the incident ray and the final direction of the emergent ray. It tells you how much the prism has turned the light from its original path.

Formula for Angle of Deviation

Key Formula

D = (i + e) - A

Where: i = angle of incidence, e = angle of emergence, A = apex angle of prism

This formula comes directly from geometry: the deviation at the first surface is (i - r₁) and at the second surface is (e - r₂). The total is:

D = (i - r₁) + (e - r₂) = (i + e) - (r₁ + r₂) = (i + e) - A

How Deviation Varies with Angle of Incidence

If you draw a graph of deviation (D) against angle of incidence (i), you get a U-shaped curve. This tells us:

  • When i is very small, D is large
  • As i increases, D decreases until it reaches a minimum (Dₐ)
  • As i increases further beyond the minimum, D increases again
  • The minimum point is called minimum deviation
Why Does Deviation Have a Minimum?

As you change the angle of incidence, you shift the path of the ray inside the prism. At exactly one angle, the ray travels parallel to the base and the geometry is perfectly symmetrical — this gives the minimum deviation.

Deviation by a Glass Slab (Rectangular Block)

When light passes through a rectangular glass slab (with parallel faces), the emergent ray is parallel to the incident ray — the deviation in direction is zero. However, the ray is laterally displaced (shifted sideways). The formula for lateral displacement is:

Lateral Displacement

d = t × sin(i - r) / cos r

Where t = thickness of glass slab, i = angle of incidence, r = angle of refraction

Real Depth and Apparent Depth

When you look at an object inside water or glass, it appears closer to the surface than it really is. This is because light from the object bends (refracts) as it passes from the denser medium (water/glass) to the less dense medium (air). The effect is called apparent depth.

Why Does It Happen?

A coin at the bottom of a pool is at the real depth. When light rays from the coin reach your eye after refracting at the water surface, they appear to come from a shallower point. That shallower point is the apparent depth. The object appears to be lifted up.

Key Formulas

n = Real Depth / Apparent Depth

Apparent Depth = Real Depth / n

Apparent Shift (how much it appears lifted) = Real Depth - Apparent Depth = Real Depth × (1 - 1/n)

Intuition Check
Water has n = 1.33. A pool 2 m deep appears only 2/1.33 = 1.5 m deep when viewed from directly above.
Glass (n = 1.5): an object 6 cm inside glass appears only 4 cm deep when viewed from air above.

Viewing from the Denser Medium

This works the other way too. If a fish looks up at an object above the water from inside the water, the object appears farther away than it really is. In this case:

From Denser Medium Looking Out

n = Apparent Depth / Real Depth (the apparent depth is now greater than real depth)

UTME / WAEC Practice — Deviation and Depth
Question 1

A coin lies at the bottom of a pond. Its real depth is 2.4 m. An observer looking from directly above sees the coin at an apparent depth of 1.8 m. Calculate the refractive index of the pond water.

n = Real Depth / Apparent Depth
n = 2.4 / 1.8
n = 1.33
Question 2

A glass block has a refractive index of 1.5 and is 9 cm thick. How far below the top surface does a scratch on the bottom appear to be when viewed from directly above?

Apparent Depth = Real Depth / n
Apparent Depth = 9 / 1.5
Apparent Depth = 6 cm
Question 3 (Hard)

A ray of light strikes a glass slab (n = 1.5, thickness = 4 cm) at an angle of incidence of 60°. Calculate: (a) the angle of refraction, and (b) the lateral displacement of the emergent ray.

(a) Snell's Law: n₁sin i = n₂sin r
1 × sin 60° = 1.5 × sin r
sin r = sin 60°/1.5 = 0.8660/1.5 = 0.5774
r = 35.26°

(b) d = t × sin(i - r)/cos r
d = 4 × sin(60° - 35.26°)/cos 35.26°
d = 4 × sin 24.74°/cos 35.26°
d = 4 × 0.4189/0.8165
d = 2.05 cm
Question 4 (Hard — WAEC)

A transparent material has a real depth of 12 cm and an apparent depth of 8 cm when viewed from air. (a) Find the refractive index. (b) Find the critical angle. (c) A ray inside this material hits a surface at 45°. Will TIR occur?

(a) n = Real/Apparent = 12/8 = 1.5

(b) sin C = 1/n = 1/1.5 = 0.667
C = 41.8° ≈ 42°

(c) 45° > 42°, so yes, TIR will occur.
Chapter 2 Summary
Angle of deviation D = (i + e) - A for a prism.
Deviation has a minimum value (Dₐ) when i = e (symmetric ray path).
A rectangular slab produces zero deviation but lateral displacement.
Objects in water appear shallower: Apparent Depth = Real Depth / n.
Refractive index = Real Depth / Apparent Depth (for viewer in air).
The apparent shift = Real Depth × (1 - 1/n).

Lenses, Lens Formula and Power of a Lens

Learning Objectives
  • Distinguish between converging and diverging lenses
  • Understand key terms: principal axis, focal point, focal length
  • Trace rays through each type of lens
  • Use the lens formula to solve problems
  • Calculate magnification
  • Define and calculate the power of a lens

Types of Lenses

A lens is a transparent curved surface that refracts light to form an image. There are two main types:

FeatureConverging (Convex) LensDiverging (Concave) Lens
ShapeThicker at the centreThinner at the centre
Effect on parallel raysBrings them to a focus (converges)Spreads them out (diverges)
Focal length (f)Positive (+)Negative (-)
Images formedReal or virtual, depending on object positionAlways virtual, upright, smaller
PowerPositiveNegative

Key Terms

  • Principal axis — the straight horizontal line through the centre of the lens
  • Optical centre (O) — the midpoint of the lens; rays through it pass straight without bending
  • Principal focus (F) — for a converging lens, the point where parallel rays meet after refraction. For a diverging lens, where they appear to come from
  • Focal length (f) — the distance from the optical centre to the principal focus
  • Focal plane — a plane perpendicular to the axis passing through F

The Three Standard Rays (Ray Diagrams)

To draw the image formed by a lens, trace any two of these three standard rays:

Three Standard Rays — Converging Lens

Ray 1: A ray parallel to the principal axis is refracted through the principal focus F on the other side.

Ray 2: A ray through the optical centre passes straight through without any bending.

Ray 3: A ray through the focal point F on the same side as the object is refracted parallel to the principal axis after passing through the lens.

Images Formed by a Converging Lens

Object PositionImage PositionNatureSize
Beyond 2FBetween F and 2F (other side)Real, invertedDiminished
At 2FAt 2F (other side)Real, invertedSame size
Between F and 2FBeyond 2F (other side)Real, invertedMagnified
At FInfinity——
Between F and OSame side as objectVirtual, uprightMagnified
A Diverging Lens Always...

...forms a virtual, upright, and diminished image, regardless of where the object is placed. The image is always between F and O on the same side as the object.

The Lens Formula

The lens formula relates the object distance (u), image distance (v), and focal length (f):

Lens Formula

1/f = 1/v - 1/u

Or equivalently: 1/f = 1/v + 1/u when the real-is-positive sign convention is used.

Sign Convention (Real-is-Positive)

  • Distances measured in the direction of incident light are positive
  • Distances against the direction of incident light are negative
  • Real objects have negative u (object is on the incoming side)
  • Real images have positive v
  • Virtual images have negative v
  • Converging lens: f is positive
  • Diverging lens: f is negative
Common Source of Errors

Different textbooks use different sign conventions. In WAEC/UTME, the most common form used is: 1/f = 1/v - 1/u (where u is negative for real objects). Always substitute with the correct sign and the answer will work out.

Magnification

Magnification Formulas

m = v / u   (linear magnification)

m = image height / object height

If m > 1, the image is larger than the object. If m < 1, it is smaller. A negative m means the image is inverted (real image).

Power of a Lens

The power (P) of a lens measures how strongly it converges or diverges light. A shorter focal length means higher power — the lens bends light more sharply.

Power Formula

P = 1 / f

Unit: Dioptre (D). f must be in metres.

Lens TypeSign of fSign of PExample
Converging (convex)Positive (+)Positive (+)f = 0.25 m → P = +4 D
Diverging (concave)Negative (-)Negative (-)f = -0.5 m → P = -2 D

Lenses in Combination

When two or more thin lenses are placed in contact, the total power is simply the sum of the individual powers:

Combined Power

PᲴᲵᲴᲰᲴ = P₁ + P₂ + P₃ + ...

UTME / WAEC Practice — Lenses
Question 1

An object is placed 30 cm from a converging lens of focal length 10 cm. Find the image distance and the magnification.

Using 1/f = 1/v - 1/u with u = -30 cm, f = +10 cm:
1/10 = 1/v - 1/(-30)
1/10 = 1/v + 1/30
1/v = 1/10 - 1/30 = 3/30 - 1/30 = 2/30
v = 15 cm (real image on other side)

m = v/u = 15/(-30) = -0.5 (inverted, half the size)
Question 2

A diverging lens has a focal length of 20 cm. An object is placed 60 cm from it. Find the image position and state its nature.

f = -20 cm, u = -60 cm
1/v = 1/f + 1/u = 1/(-20) + 1/(-60)
1/v = -3/60 - 1/60 = -4/60
v = -15 cm

Negative v means virtual, upright, diminished image on the same side as the object, 15 cm from the lens.
Question 3 (Hard)

A converging lens forms a real image three times the size of the object. The image is 45 cm from the lens. Find: (a) the object distance, (b) the focal length, (c) the power of the lens.

(a) m = v/u → -3 = 45/u (negative because real image is inverted)
u = 45/(-3) = -15 cm (object is 15 cm from lens)

(b) 1/f = 1/v - 1/u = 1/45 - 1/(-15) = 1/45 + 1/15 = 1/45 + 3/45 = 4/45
f = 11.25 cm = 0.1125 m

(c) P = 1/f = 1/0.1125 = +8.89 D
Question 4 (Hard — UTME)

A converging lens of power +5 D is placed in contact with a diverging lens of power -2 D. An object is placed 25 cm from the combination. Find the image distance.

Combined power: P = +5 + (-2) = +3 D
f = 1/P = 1/3 m = 33.3 cm

Lens formula: u = -25 cm, f = +33.3 cm
1/v = 1/f + 1/u = 1/33.3 + 1/(-25)
1/v = 0.03003 - 0.04 = -0.00997
v = -100.3 cm ≈ -100 cm

Image is virtual, 100 cm on the same side as the object.
Chapter 4

Optical Instruments and The Human Eye

Learning Objectives
  • Understand the simple magnifier and its use
  • Describe the compound microscope and calculate magnification
  • Describe the telescope (astronomical and Galilean)
  • Understand the camera and how it works
  • Understand the projector
  • Describe the human eye and its parts
  • Understand defects of vision and their corrections

The Simple Magnifier (Magnifying Glass)

A simple magnifier is a single converging lens used to produce a magnified virtual image of a small object. The object is placed between the lens and its focal point.

Angular Magnification

For the image at the near point (25 cm from the eye):

M = 1 + D/f

For the image at infinity (relaxed eye):

M = D/f

D = least distance of distinct vision = 25 cm for a normal eye, f = focal length of lens

The Compound Microscope

A compound microscope uses two converging lenses to produce a very highly magnified image of a tiny object:

  • Objective lens — short focal length; object is placed just beyond 2F₀; forms a real, inverted, magnified intermediate image
  • Eyepiece lens — acts as a magnifying glass; magnifies the intermediate image further
Microscope Magnification

M = m₀ × Mₑ

Where m₀ = linear magnification by objective, Mₑ = angular magnification by eyepiece

The Astronomical Telescope

An astronomical telescope uses two converging lenses to view distant objects:

  • Objective lens — large diameter, long focal length (f₀); gathers light and forms a real image at its focus
  • Eyepiece lens — short focal length (fₑ); magnifies the image formed by the objective
  • In normal adjustment, the lenses are separated by (f₀ + fₑ) and the final image is at infinity
Telescope Magnification (Normal Adjustment)

M = f₀ / fₑ

The Galilean Telescope

Uses a converging objective and a diverging eyepiece. It produces an upright final image and is shorter than the astronomical telescope. Used in opera glasses and some binoculars. Its magnification is the same formula: M = f₀ / |fₑ|.

The Camera

A camera uses a converging lens to form a real, inverted, diminished image on a sensor or film. The key features are:

  • Aperture (f-number) — controls the amount of light entering. A smaller f-number (wider aperture) lets in more light
  • Shutter speed — controls how long light hits the sensor
  • Focusing — the lens is moved to adjust v so a sharp image forms on the film/sensor
UTME / WAEC Practice — Optical Instruments
Question 1

A magnifying glass has a focal length of 5 cm. Calculate its magnifying power (a) when the image is at the near point (25 cm) and (b) when the image is at infinity.

(a) M = 1 + D/f = 1 + 25/5 = 1 + 5 = 6

(b) M = D/f = 25/5 = 5
Question 2

An astronomical telescope has an objective lens of focal length 80 cm and an eyepiece of focal length 4 cm. Find: (a) the magnification in normal adjustment, and (b) the length of the telescope.

(a) M = f₀/fₑ = 80/4 = 20

(b) L = f₀ + fₑ = 80 + 4 = 84 cm

A compound microscope has an objective of focal length 1.0 cm and an eyepiece of focal length 5 cm. The object is placed 1.2 cm from the objective. Calculate: (a) the image distance from the objective, (b) the linear magnification by the objective, (c) the angular magnification by the eyepiece (image at near point), and (d) the total magnification.

(a) 1/v = 1/f₀ + 1/u = 1/1 + 1/(-1.2)
1/v = 1 - 0.833 = 0.167

(b) m₀ = v/u = 6/1.2 = 5

A telescope in normal adjustment gives a magnification of 30. The length of the telescope is 62 cm. Find the focal lengths of the objective and the eyepiece.

M = f₀/fₑ = 30 → f₀ = 30fₑ
L = f₀ + fₑ = 62
30fₑ + fₑ = 62
31fₑ = 62
fₑ = 2 cm
f₀ = 60 cm

The Human Eye

The human eye is an incredible natural optical instrument. It forms a real, inverted image on the retina and the brain interprets this as upright. Understanding it in physics means knowing its parts, how it focuses, and how it can go wrong.

Parts of the Eye and Their Functions

Accommodation

Accommodation is the ability of the eye to adjust its focal length to focus on objects at different distances by changing the shape of the lens.

  • Viewing near objects: ciliary muscles contract, lens becomes fatter (more curved), focal length decreases, power increases
  • Viewing far objects: ciliary muscles relax, lens becomes flatter (less curved), focal length increases, power decreases

Near Point and Far Point

  • Near point: the closest point at which the eye can see clearly. For a normal eye this is about 25 cm. Also called the least distance of distinct vision (D)
  • Far point: the furthest point at which the eye can see clearly. For a normal eye this is infinity

UTME / WAEC Practice — The Human Eye
Question 1

A short-sighted person cannot see objects clearly beyond 50 cm. What type and power of lens is needed to correct their vision so they can see objects at infinity?

The person's far point is 50 cm = 0.5 m. A diverging lens is needed to make an object at infinity appear to come from 50 cm (the far point).

Using 1/f = 1/v - 1/u: image must be at v = -0.5 m (virtual), object at u = -infinity (infinity)
1/f = 1/(-0.5) - 1/(-infinity) = -2 - 0 = -2
f = -0.5 m → Power = -2 D
A diverging lens of power -2 D is needed.
Question 2

A long-sighted person has a near point of 100 cm. What power of lens will enable them to read a book held at 25 cm?

The lens must form a virtual image of the book (at 25 cm) at the near point (100 cm).
u = -25 cm = -0.25 m, v = -100 cm = -1.0 m (virtual image on same side)
1/f = 1/v - 1/u = 1/(-1.0) - 1/(-0.25) = -1 + 4 = +3
P = +3 D (converging lens)
Question 3 (Hard)

A myopic eye has its far point 40 cm away. The person reads a newspaper at 25 cm without glasses. With corrective glasses, they should be able to read at the normal near point (25 cm) and see to infinity. (a) Find the corrective lens power. (b) With this corrective lens, where is the near point now?

(a) Corrective lens moves far point from 40 cm to infinity:
v = -40 cm = -0.4 m, u = -infinity
1/f = 1/(-0.4) - 0 = -2.5
P = -2.5 D (diverging lens)

(b) With glasses, find new near point. The eye's near point without glasses is 25 cm. The glasses must form the image at 25 cm when object is at some distance x:
v = -25 cm, f = -40 cm
1/u = 1/v - 1/f = 1/(-25) - 1/(-40) = -4/100 + 2.5/100 = -1.5/100
u = -66.7 cm
New near point = 66.7 cm (further away than before — a trade-off of myopia correction)

What Is White Light?

White light is not a single colour. It is a mixture of many different colours, each with its own wavelength. When white light passes through a prism, the colours separate because each wavelength is refracted by a slightly different amount. This separation is called dispersion.

Dispersion by a Prism

When white light passes through a triangular prism, it produces a spectrum — a band of colours arranged in order of wavelength. The reason different colours separate is that the refractive index of glass is different for each wavelength. Violet light is refracted the most; red light is refracted the least.

Recombining the Spectrum

If the separated colours are passed through a second prism (inverted), they recombine to give white light again. This proves that white light is a mixture of all these colours. Newton was the first to demonstrate this.

Chromatic Aberration

Because different colours are refracted by different amounts, a lens focuses different colours at slightly different points. This causes coloured fringes around images — called chromatic aberration. It is corrected by using an achromatic doublet (two lenses of different glass types cemented together).

Colour Mixing

There are two completely different types of colour mixing: additive (mixing coloured lights) and subtractive (mixing pigments or filters).

Additive Colour Mixing (Coloured Lights)

The three primary colours of light are: Red, Green, Blue (RGB). When added together in equal proportions, they produce white light.

Pigments and filters work by absorbing (subtracting) certain colours from white light and reflecting or transmitting the rest. The primary colours for pigment mixing are: Cyan, Magenta, Yellow (CMY).

The Colour of an Object

An object appears a certain colour because of which wavelengths it reflects or absorbs:

  • A red object in white light reflects red and absorbs all other colours
  • A white object reflects all colours equally
  • A black object absorbs all colours and reflects none
  • A red object viewed in blue light appears black — because it has no red to reflect

Filters

A colour filter transmits only its own colour and absorbs the rest. For example, a green filter placed in front of white light transmits only green and blocks all other colours.

Wave Motion and Properties of Waves

What Is a Wave?

A wave is a disturbance that transfers energy from one place to another without permanently transferring the medium itself. When a wave passes through a medium, the particles of the medium vibrate about their equilibrium position — they do not travel with the wave. Only the energy travels.

Types of Waves

Transverse Waves

The particles of the medium vibrate perpendicular (at 90°) to the direction of wave travel. Examples: light waves, water ripples, radio waves, all electromagnetic waves.

Longitudinal Waves

The particles of the medium vibrate parallel (in the same direction) to the direction of wave travel. This creates alternating regions of compression and rarefaction. Examples: sound waves, seismic P-waves.

Key Wave Terms

  • Amplitude (A) — the maximum displacement of a particle from its equilibrium position. It determines the energy/loudness of the wave.
  • Wavelength (λ) — the distance between two successive points in phase (e.g., crest to crest or compression to compression). Unit: metres (m).
  • Period (T) — the time taken for one complete vibration (one full cycle). Unit: seconds (s).
  • Frequency (f) — the number of complete vibrations per second. Unit: Hertz (Hz).
  • Wave speed (v) — the speed at which the wave profile (the pattern of crests and troughs) moves through the medium.
Key Relationships

f = 1/T   (frequency and period are reciprocals)

v = fλ   (wave speed = frequency × wavelength)

Properties of Waves

All waves — whether sound, light, or water — share the same set of properties: reflection, refraction, diffraction, interference, and superposition.

1. Reflection

When a wave hits a barrier, it bounces back. The angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection. Examples: echoes (sound), mirrors (light), radar.

2. Refraction

When a wave moves from one medium to another, its speed changes. If it hits the boundary at an angle, its direction also changes. The frequency never changes during refraction — only speed and wavelength change.

Snell's Law for Waves

n = sin i / sin r = v₁ / v₂ = λ₁ / λ₂

3. Diffraction

When a wave passes through a gap or around an obstacle, it spreads out. Diffraction is most noticeable when the gap size is approximately equal to the wavelength of the wave. This is why we can hear sound around corners (long wavelength) but not see around corners (very short wavelength of light).

4. Interference

When two waves meet, they superpose (add up). This is called interference:

  • Constructive interference: two waves in phase add up to give a larger amplitude. Crests meet crests.
  • Destructive interference: two waves out of phase cancel each other. Crests meet troughs. The result can be zero displacement.

5. Superposition

The principle of superposition states that when two or more waves meet, the resultant displacement at any point is the algebraic sum of the individual displacements at that point.

6. Polarisation

Polarisation is the restriction of a transverse wave's vibrations to one plane only. Only transverse waves can be polarised — longitudinal waves cannot. Light from the sun vibrates in all planes; a polaroid filter allows only one plane through. This is used in sunglasses and LCD screens.

The Nature of Sound

Sound is a longitudinal, mechanical wave. It is produced by vibrating objects and requires a material medium (solid, liquid, or gas) to travel through. It cannot travel through a vacuum.

Speed of Sound

Sound travels at different speeds in different media:

MediumApproximate Speed
Air (at 0°C)331 m/s
Air (at 20°C)343 m/s
Water~1500 m/s
Steel~5000 m/s

Sound travels faster in denser, more elastic media. Solids > Liquids > Gases.

Speed of sound increases with temperature: for every 1°C rise, speed increases by about 0.6 m/s.

Characteristics of Sound

  • Loudness — determined by amplitude. Greater amplitude = greater energy = louder sound. Measured in decibels (dB).
  • Pitch — determined by frequency. Higher frequency = higher pitch. Humans hear between about 20 Hz and 20,000 Hz.
  • Quality (Timbre) — determined by the waveform (the mix of harmonics). A violin and a piano playing the same note differ in quality/timbre.
Infrasound and Ultrasound

Infrasound: frequency below 20 Hz. Used by elephants and whales to communicate over long distances.

Ultrasound: frequency above 20,000 Hz (20 kHz). Used in medical scanning (sonography), sonar, and cleaning equipment.

Stationary (Standing) Waves

When two identical waves travelling in opposite directions meet in the same medium, they superpose to form a stationary wave — a pattern of nodes and antinodes that does not move forward.

  • Node: a point of zero displacement. Destructive interference always occurs here.
  • Antinode: a point of maximum displacement. Constructive interference always occurs here.
  • The distance between two adjacent nodes (or antinodes) is λ/2.
Stationary vs Progressive Waves

Progressive wave: energy and wave pattern both move forward.

Stationary wave: pattern does not move; energy is stored between nodes; all particles between two nodes vibrate in phase but with different amplitudes.

Vibrating Strings

When a string is fixed at both ends and plucked, stationary waves form. The ends are always nodes (because the string is fixed). The simplest pattern is one loop (antinode in the middle) — this is the fundamental mode (first harmonic).

f₁ = (1/2L) × √(T/μ)

Laws of Vibrating Strings

The fundamental frequency of a vibrating string:

  • Is inversely proportional to its length: f ∝ 1/L
  • Is directly proportional to the square root of tension: f ∝ √T
  • Is inversely proportional to the square root of mass per unit length: f ∝ 1/√μ

A guitarist increases pitch by: pressing a fret (shortening L), tightening a peg (increasing T), or using a thinner string (decreasing μ). All three raise frequency according to the formula above.

End Correction

In practice, an antinode at an open end forms slightly beyond the end of the pipe. This extra length is called the end correction (e). A common approximation is e = 0.3d, where d is the internal diameter of the pipe. For an open pipe, the effective length is L + 2e; for a closed pipe, L + e.

Resonance

Resonance occurs when an object is made to vibrate at its own natural frequency by the periodic force of another vibrating object. At resonance, the amplitude of vibration becomes very large.

Examples of Resonance
A tuning fork held near a resonance tube — when the tube length matches the wavelength, the air column resonates and a loud sound is heard.
A swing pushed at its natural frequency — the amplitude increases with each push.
The Tacoma Narrows Bridge (1940) — collapsed because wind resonated with the bridge's natural frequency.

The Resonance Tube Experiment

A closed pipe (resonance tube) is used to measure the speed of sound in air. A vibrating tuning fork is held over the open end. The water level is lowered until a loud resonance is heard.

  • First resonance: L₁ = λ/4 (approximately)
  • Second resonance: L₂ = 3λ/4
  • So: L₂ - L₁ = λ/2 → λ = 2(L₂ - L₁)
  • Speed: v = fλ = 2f(L₂ - L₁)

Hard Theory Questions — Homologous Series & Naming of Hydrocarbons
Question 1

Explain, using structural (bonding) principles, why alkanes are called "saturated" hydrocarbons while alkenes and alkynes are called "unsaturated". Relate your answer directly to the type of bonding present.

Enter code to view answer
Incorrect code. Please try again.
A hydrocarbon is "saturated" when every carbon atom is joined to its neighbours using only single covalent bonds (C–C). Since each carbon can form only four bonds in total, and all four are already used on single bonds to hydrogen or other carbons, the molecule is holding the maximum possible number of hydrogen atoms — saturated with hydrogen, like a sponge that has soaked up as much water as it can. Alkanes are the saturated hydrocarbons, following the general formula CnH2n+2.

A hydrocarbon is "unsaturated" when it contains at least one carbon-to-carbon double bond (C=C, alkenes) or triple bond (C≡C, alkynes). Because two (or three) of a carbon's four bonds are used joining to the same neighbouring carbon, fewer bonds remain for hydrogen, so the molecule carries fewer hydrogens than the maximum possible — it is not "full" of hydrogen.

This structural difference is exactly why unsaturated hydrocarbons undergo addition reactions (the double/triple bond breaks, letting new atoms attach, making the molecule saturated), while saturated alkanes cannot — there is no spare bonding capacity for anything to add on to. Alkanes can only react by substitution (swapping a hydrogen for another atom), never addition.
Question 2

Members of a homologous series differ by a repeating –CH2– unit yet show a gradual, predictable rise in boiling point. Explain, in terms of intermolecular forces, why boiling point increases steadily as the number of carbon atoms increases.

Enter code to view answer
Incorrect code. Please try again.
As chain length increases: (1) molecular size and surface area increase, giving neighbouring molecules more area of contact; (2) the total van der Waals forces (weak, temporary attractions caused by momentary uneven electron distribution creating tiny dipoles) between molecules grow stronger — not because each individual attraction is stronger, but because more atoms means more points of contact adding up to more total attraction.

Boiling requires enough energy to completely separate molecules from each other. A longer molecule with stronger total van der Waals forces needs more heat energy to pull its molecules apart into a gas. This is why boiling point rises steadily and predictably up a homologous series — each extra –CH2– unit adds a small, fairly constant amount of extra surface contact, and therefore a small, fairly constant rise in boiling point. This gradual, predictable change in physical properties (while chemical behaviour stays similar, since it's governed by the same type of bonding/functional group) is one of the defining features of a homologous series.
Question 3

Explain the IUPAC rules used to name a branched alkane, using 2,3-dimethylbutane as a worked illustrative example, and explain why systematic naming is preferred over trivial/common names.

Enter code to view answer
Incorrect code. Please try again.
Rule 1: Find the longest continuous carbon chain — this is the "parent chain" and gives the base name (e.g. 4 carbons = "butane").
Rule 2: Number the carbons from whichever end gives the lowest possible numbers to the branches.
Rule 3: Name each branch (a 1-carbon branch = "methyl", 2-carbon = "ethyl").
Rule 4: If a branch appears more than once, use "di-", "tri-", "tetra-" and give a locant number for each occurrence.
Rule 5: List different substituents alphabetically in the final name, with locant numbers.

Worked example — 2,3-dimethylbutane: "Butane" = a 4-carbon parent chain. "Dimethyl" = two methyl (–CH3) branches. "2,3-" = those branches are attached to carbon 2 and carbon 3 of the butane chain.

Why IUPAC names are preferred: common names (like "isobutane") were given historically with no fixed rules, so each one had to be memorised individually — and this becomes impossible once you consider that millions of organic compounds exist. IUPAC names are built directly from the compound's actual structure using a small, fixed set of rules. Once you know the rules, you can name (or draw) any hydrocarbon correctly — even one you've never seen — and any chemist anywhere will understand exactly the same structure from that one name.
Question 4

Distinguish between chain isomerism, position isomerism, and functional group isomerism, giving one hydrocarbon example of each.

Enter code to view answer
Incorrect code. Please try again.
Structural isomers share the same molecular formula but have different arrangements of atoms.

1. Chain isomerism — the carbon skeleton is arranged differently (straight vs branched). Example: butane (straight 4-carbon chain) and 2-methylpropane (a branched 3-carbon chain with a methyl group) are both C4H10.

2. Position isomerism — the skeleton stays the same, but a double bond (or group) is attached at a different position. Example: but-1-ene (CH2=CH–CH2–CH3) and but-2-ene (CH3–CH=CH–CH3) are both C4H8, same straight chain, double bond in a different place.

3. Functional group isomerism — the same molecular formula represents compounds from entirely different homologous series. Example: but-1-ene (an alkene, C4H8) and cyclobutane (a cycloalkane, C4H8) share the same formula, but one has a reactive C=C double bond while the other is a saturated ring.

The unifying idea: same formula, different structure, and (usually) different properties.
Question 5

Explain why alkenes are generally more chemically reactive than alkanes, relating your answer to bond energy and the mechanism of addition reactions.

Enter code to view answer
Incorrect code. Please try again.
A C=C double bond is made of one strong sigma (σ) bond, like the single bonds in alkanes, plus one weaker pi (π) bond formed from sideways overlap of p-orbitals above and below the plane of the carbons. The π bond's electron density is exposed above and below the molecule, unlike a σ bond's electrons which are tightly and symmetrically shared directly between the two nuclei.

Because the π bond is weaker (roughly 250 kJ/mol vs roughly 350 kJ/mol for a σ bond) and its electrons sit more loosely and openly, it is far easier to break, and it strongly attracts electron-seeking (electrophilic) reagents such as bromine (Br2) or hydrogen halides (HBr).

This is why alkenes readily undergo addition reactions: the weak π bond breaks, freeing one bond on each carbon, which then bonds to the two incoming atoms — converting the double bond into a single bond and turning the alkene into a saturated product. Alkanes, having only strong σ bonds with no exposed electron-rich region and no spare bonding capacity, cannot do this; they only react under harsher conditions (like UV light with halogens) via slow substitution, one hydrogen at a time. This bonding difference is the fundamental reason unsaturated hydrocarbons are more reactive than saturated ones.
